Sentence Boundaries & Conjunctions — Quick Reference

Three ways sentences break, and how coordinating/subordinating conjunctions fix or connect them

Error 1

Run-On

Two independent clauses joined with no punctuation at all. Fix: add a period, semicolon, or coordinating conjunction + comma.

Error 2

Comma Splice

Two independent clauses joined with only a comma. Fix: replace the comma with a period, semicolon, or add a conjunction after it.

Error 3

Fragment

A group of words punctuated as a sentence but missing a subject, verb, or both — or a dependent clause standing alone.

Coordinating

FANBOYS

For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So — join two independent clauses with a comma before the conjunction.

Subordinating

Dependent Clause Words

Although, because, since, while, when, after, before — create a dependent clause that cannot stand alone.

Logic Check

Match the Relationship

Contrast needs although/but/yet. Cause needs because/since. Don't just pick a grammatically legal word — pick the one that matches the actual logic.

Syra's Method

1
Read each clause separately. Cover the first clause and read only the second — is it a complete sentence? Both complete = you need more than a comma.
2
Name the error before choosing a fix. "This is a comma splice" → eliminate any answer that leaves two independent clauses joined by only a comma.
3
For conjunction questions, find the logical relationship first. Contrast, cause, or addition? Then pick the conjunction that matches — don't just pick one that sounds fine.
Top Traps
Comma splice mistaken for correct (comma feels natural)
Fragment after a long noun phrase that sounds complete
A grammatically legal conjunction that reverses the actual logic (e.g. "although" where the sentence needs "because")
Semicolon placed before a subordinating conjunction (wrong — semicolons only join independent clauses)
