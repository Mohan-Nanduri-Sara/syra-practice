The SAT dual-text question: structure and traps

The Pattern

Always the same structure

Text 1 makes a claim or argument. Text 2 responds to it — agreeing, disagreeing, complicating, or qualifying. The question asks how Author 2 would respond to Author 1.

Type 1

Disagreement

Author 2 directly contradicts or challenges the central claim of Author 1. Look for evidence that directly refutes or complicates the main point.

Type 2

Partial Agreement / Qualification

Author 2 accepts part of Author 1's claim but adds a condition, limitation, or complication. "Yes, but..." structure.

Type 3

Support / Extension

Author 2 provides evidence or argument that strengthens or extends Author 1's claim.

Trap 1

Answering the wrong question

The question asks how Author 2 responds to Author 1 — not what Author 2 says in isolation.

Trap 2

Too strong / too weak

Wrong answers often exaggerate the agreement or disagreement. The correct answer matches the actual relationship.

Syra's Method

1
Summarize Text 1 in one sentence. What is Author 1's central claim? Write it down or underline it. This is what you're looking for a response to.
2
Summarize Text 2 in one sentence. What is Author 2's main point? How does it relate to what Author 1 said? Agree? Disagree? Complicate?
3
Select the answer that describes the relationship accurately. Wrong answers will misstate the direction (support vs. challenge), misstate the strength, or confuse topic with argument. Match the relationship, not just the topic.
Top Traps
Wrong direction (support vs. challenge)
Too strong or too weak
About a related topic but not the specific claim
Confuses what Author 2 says with how Author 2 responds to Author 1

The "broken windows" theory of policing, introduced by James Q. Wilson and George Kelling in 1982, argues that visible signs of disorder — graffiti, broken windows, loitering — signal that a community does not care about its environment and invite further crime. Studies examining cities that adopted zero-tolerance policies have found that crime reductions coincided with broader economic improvements and demographic changes, making it impossible to isolate the effect of policing style. More troublingly, the policies were disproportionately applied to communities of color, resulting in mass stops, arrests, and prosecutions for minor violations — with significant collateral damage to community trust in law enforcement.

Neuroscientist David Eagleman argues that the conscious mind receives only a small fraction of the information processed by the brain — most of what shapes perception, decision-making, and behavior occurs below the threshold of awareness. This has profound implications for the concept of free will: if most of our mental activity is unconscious, our sense of consciously choosing our actions may be largely illusory.

Even if many cognitive processes are unconscious, this does not entail that no deliberative decision-making is genuinely volitional. Neuroscientist Uri Maoz and philosopher Alfred Mele have argued that the experimental paradigms used to challenge free will — particularly Libet's readiness potential studies — do not actually test the kinds of complex, sustained deliberative choices that constitute the free will most people care about.

Behavioral economists Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein introduced the concept of the "nudge" — small changes to the choice architecture of decisions that steer people toward better outcomes without restricting freedom of choice. Automatic enrollment in retirement savings plans, placing healthier foods at eye level in cafeterias, and organ donation opt-out systems are all examples of nudges that have been shown to significantly improve outcomes with minimal coercion.
Text 2
Critics of nudge theory argue that the concept, while superficially appealing, carries embedded paternalism. Every nudge encodes a judgment about what constitutes a "better" outcome — a judgment made by policymakers or designers rather than by the individuals being nudged. This is particularly problematic in domains where preferences are legitimately diverse, and where designating one option as the default implicitly delegitimizes other choices. Transparency about nudge design, critics argue, partially undermines its effectiveness.

Philosopher Peter Singer's "effective altruism" movement argues that individuals in wealthy countries have a moral obligation to donate a significant portion of their income to the most effective charitable interventions — those that can be demonstrated through rigorous evidence to save the most lives per dollar. Singer's utilitarian framework holds that geography is morally irrelevant: allowing a distant person to die when you could prevent it at modest personal cost is morally equivalent to allowing someone in front of you to drown.
Text 2
Critics of effective altruism argue that the movement's emphasis on measurable, scalable interventions systematically disadvantages the complex, long-term work of structural change — advocacy, organizing, and policy reform — which is harder to quantify but may address the root causes of poverty and disease rather than their symptoms. A world in which wealthy individuals choose which global problems deserve resources, based on metrics that favor tractable near-term interventions, may perpetuate the global power imbalances that generate those problems in the first place.
