Punctuation — Quick Reference

Five marks, five clear jobs — know which one the sentence actually needs

Commas

Nonessential = Commas, Essential = No Commas

"Yellowstone, WHICH WAS ESTABLISHED IN 1872, is..." (nonessential, set off). "The book THAT EVERYONE RECOMMENDED was..." (essential, no commas).

Semicolons

Join Two Independent Clauses

"The museum was closed; the tour was rescheduled." Both sides must be able to stand alone as complete sentences.

Colons

Only After a Complete Independent Clause

"The chef needed three ingredients: flour, eggs, and butter." Never right after a linking verb like "were" or "is."

Dashes

Must Come in Matching Pairs

"The results—remarkably consistent—convinced the skeptics." Never mix one dash with one comma to bracket the same phrase.

Apostrophes

Possessive vs Contraction

ITS vs IT'S, WHOSE vs WHO'S, THEIR vs THERE'S — these sound identical but mean completely different things.

Plural Possessives

Add ' After the S

Regular plural: researchers' (the researchers own it). Irregular plural: men's, women's, children's (add 's, not just ').

Syra's Method

1
Identify what's on both sides of the punctuation mark. Two complete sentences? Semicolon or period. One complete + one fragment? Comma or colon, depending on the relationship.
2
For colons, check that what comes before is a complete independent clause. If you could put a period there instead, a colon works. If not, the colon is wrong.
3
For apostrophes, say the sentence out loud replacing the word. "Who's" = "who is" — if that doesn't make sense, you need "whose" instead.
Top Traps
Commas added around an essential (restrictive) clause that should have none
A colon placed directly after a linking verb like "were" or "is"
Mismatched dash-and-comma pair instead of two matching dashes
Regular vs. irregular plural possessives confused (researchers' vs. men's)
