Words in Context — Quick Reference

Three types of vocabulary question the SAT tests — including literary style

Type 1

Completing a blank

A blank mid-passage must be filled with the word that best fits the surrounding logic — not just any word that sounds good.

Type 2

Most logical & precise

The SAT gives four plausible words. The right one matches both meaning AND tone exactly.

Type 3

Contrast / Similarity

Watch for contrast signals (although, despite, however) and similarity signals (similarly, likewise).

Watch for

Positive vs. Negative

A word can have the right general meaning but the wrong charge.

Watch for

Register & Precision

"Said" vs. "asserted" vs. "conceded" — each implies a different relationship between speaker and claim.

Literary Mode

Tone Runs Deeper in Fiction

In novel excerpts, the blank often carries a character's emotional or psychological state, not just a factual meaning — read for voice, not just logic.

Syra's Method

1
Read the full sentence. Never read just the blank. The rest of the sentence tells you what the blank must do.
2
Predict your own word. Before looking at choices, decide: positive/negative? action/state? This protects you from attractive wrong answers.
3
In literary passages, predict tone as well as meaning. A novel excerpt's blank often needs a word that matches the character's emotional register, not just a dictionary-correct synonym.
Top Traps
Familiar-sounding word chosen over the precise one
Wrong charge (positive/negative) even when the general meaning is close
Near-synonyms with different precision or register
In literary passages: a word that fits the plot facts but not the character's tone
