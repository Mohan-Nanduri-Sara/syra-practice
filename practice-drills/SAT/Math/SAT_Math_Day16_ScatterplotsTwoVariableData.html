Scatterplots & Two-Variable Data — SAT Reference

Core rules you must know cold for the data analysis unit

Key Facts

Line of best fity = mx + b, fit to minimize total squared distance to points
Residualactual y − predicted y
Interpolationpredicting within the range of the original data
Extrapolationpredicting outside the range of the original data (riskier)
Correlation strengthhow tightly points cluster around the line, NOT the slope's steepness
Two-way tableconditional % = cell value ÷ the relevant row/column total
Slope & Intercept in Context

Match each number to what it actually measures

The slope is the rate of change (units of y per unit of x). The y-intercept is the predicted y-value when x = 0 — don't swap which is which when interpreting a real-world model.

Residuals

Always actual minus predicted

A positive residual means the actual point is ABOVE the line of best fit; a negative residual means it's below. The point with the largest |residual| is the one farthest from the line.

Correlation Strength vs. Direction

These are two separate ideas

Direction (positive/negative) describes which way the trend goes. Strength describes how tightly the points cluster around the line — a steep slope isn't automatically "strong."

Extrapolation Risk

Predicting outside the data range is less reliable

A line of best fit is only tested within the range of the original data. Using it to predict far outside that range assumes the trend continues, which isn't guaranteed.

Correlation Is Not Causation

An association doesn't prove one thing causes another

Two variables can be strongly correlated because of a third factor, coincidence, or reverse causation — a scatterplot alone never proves a causal claim.

Watch Out For
Swapping the meaning of slope and y-intercept when interpreting a model
Confusing a steep slope with a "strong" correlation
Using the wrong total (row vs. column vs. grand total) in a two-way table percentage
Concluding causation from a scatterplot that only shows correlation
